Project 3

Fun with Flow Models

Avery Lee · October 20, 2026

Flow models generate images by learning a velocity field that carries random noise to natural images. During training, a noise sample and a real image are joined by a straight path, and a network learns to predict the direction of travel at any point along it. To generate, the model starts from pure noise and follows its predicted velocity in small steps until it arrives at an image. Because every point along the way is a partly noisy image, the same model can also denoise photos, edit existing images, and combine predictions from several prompts.

Part A explores these abilities with a large pretrained text-to-image flow model. Part B builds and trains a flow-matching model from scratch. Every result is available here.

Part AExploring the Power of Flow Models

A pretrained text-to-image flow model, PixNerd-XXL-P16-T2I, is used to denoise, generate and edit images and to create optical illusions. No training is required for this section.

Part A · 1.1Constructing Noisy Training Inputs

A noisy training input is a straight-line mix of Gaussian noise \(\epsilon\) and the clean image \(x_1\):

$$x_t=(1-t)\,\epsilon+t\,x_1,\qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$$

At \(t=0\) the input is pure noise, and at \(t=1\) it is the clean image. The image is also scaled by \(t\).

At \(t=0.25\) only the dark feet and the rough outline survive the noise. By \(t=0.75\) the face and the fur textures are clear.

Part A · 1.2Classical Denoising

Gaussian blur is the baseline that needs no learned model. The image inside \(x_t\) is scaled by \(t\), so it is first divided by \(t\) to restore its brightness, then blurred with a Gaussian filter \(G_\sigma\):

$$\frac{x_t}{t}=x_1+\frac{1-t}{t}\,\epsilon,\qquad \hat{x}_1^{\text{blur}}=G_\sigma\!\left(\frac{x_t}{t}\right)$$

Dividing by \(t\) also scales up the noise, so at small \(t\) the noise overwhelms the image. For each input, the result with the lowest mean squared error to the clean image out of \(\sigma\in\{1,2,4,8,16\}\) was kept.

Part A · 1.3One-Step Denoising

The pretrained model predicts the velocity \(v_\theta(x_t,t,c)\) at a noisy image. A single prediction can be extrapolated all the way to \(t=1\):

$$\hat{x}_1=x_t+(1-t)\,v_\theta(x_t,t,c)$$

The condition \(c\) is the embedding of the prompt “a high quality photo”.

This beats simply blurring. At \(t=0.75\) the estimate is nearly identical to the original. At \(t=0.25\) the pose, colors and background are similar, but the texture is extremely smooth. A single step from heavy noise lands near the average of all the images that could have produced that input, and an average is blurry.

Part A · 1.4Visualizing Velocity

Noisy input, predicted update, ground-truth update and error map at each \(t\)

In 1.3 the model added \((1-t)\hat v_t\) to the noisy image, where \(\hat v_t=v_\theta(x_t,t,c)\). Because the noise was saved, the update that recovers the image exactly is also known. With \(v_{\text{gt}}=x_1-\epsilon\),

$$(1-t)\,v_{\text{gt}}=x_1-x_t.$$

The error heatmap is the root-mean-square (RMS) difference between the two updates across RGB at each pixel:

$$E_t=\sqrt{\operatorname{mean}_{\text{RGB}}\!\left(\big[(1-t)(\hat v_t-v_{\text{gt}})\big]^2\right)}$$

The error maps share a fixed linear scale, where brighter means a larger error. The error is highest on edges and fine texture, such as the donkey's outline, the fur and the edge of the napkin holder behind it, and close to zero on flat areas like the floor. It shrinks as \(t\) grows, because there is less noise left to remove and the update is scaled by \(1-t\).

Part A · 1.5Iterative Denoising with Euler Sampling

Only doing one step produces inaccurate outputs because the velocity changes along the path. Euler integration takes a small step, predicts a new velocity at the new point, and repeats:

$$x_{t+h}=x_t+h\,v_\theta(x_t,t,c)$$

Starting from the saved \(t=0.50\) input, the sampler takes 25 steps of \(h=0.02\) with “a high quality photo”. By \(t=0.80\) the last steps bring back sharp fur and a smooth floor. Euler denoising looks the most like a real photo: the fur and the stitching come back. One-step is close to the original but smoother, and blurring is much worse than both.

Quality is poor: seed 180 has no face, seed 183's face is distorted, the skin in seeds 181 and 183 has strange textures, and seed 182 has a malformed hand. Part A · 2.1Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG)

To make samples follow the prompt more closely, CFG evaluates the model twice at the same \(x_t\) and \(t\): once with the prompt (\(v_c\)) and once with an empty prompt "" (\(v_u\)). It then pushes the velocity further in the direction the prompt adds:

$$v_{\text{cfg}}=v_c+w\,(v_c-v_u)$$

These samples use \(w=7\). With \(w=0\) the formula reduces to \(v_c\), and in the notebook it gives exactly the same output as the conditional prediction.

CFG fixes the previous issues with skin texture and form. All five are sharp, centered studio headshots with plain backgrounds. Some of the layout carries over from the shared noise. Seed 182 still has a hand on the cheek, and seed 180 is still turned slightly to the left.

However, there is not much diversity in the outputs. With CFG, every sample is the same kind of frontal portrait.

Part A · 3.3Visual Anagrams

Each pair: upright (left) and rotated 180° (right)

A visual anagram looks like one prompt upright and another upside down. At each step the CFG velocity is computed for prompt \(p_1\) on \(x_t\), and for prompt \(p_2\) on \(x_t\) rotated 180°. The second velocity is rotated back and the two are averaged:

$$\begin{aligned}v_1&=\text{CFG}(x_t,t,p_1)\\ v_2&=\text{flip}\big(\text{CFG}(\text{flip}(x_t),t,p_2)\big)\\ v&=\tfrac{1}{2}(v_1+v_2)\end{aligned}$$

'flip' here rotates the image by 180°.

An oil painting of an old man / an oil painting of people around a campfire. Upright, the glow of the fire becomes the light on the old man's forehead, and the people around the fire become the folds of his face. Rotated, it is a group sitting around a campfire at night.

A lithograph of a snowy mountain village / a lithograph of a horse. Upright, a village in the snow under a mountain. Rotated, a horse whose body is drawn with the village's roofs and fences.

A charcoal sketch of a duck / a charcoal sketch of an elephant. Upright, a duck. Rotated, a small elephant with large ears.

An oil painting of houses / an oil painting of a bouquet of flowers. Upright, houses whose roofs and walls are made of flower petals, with trees in the back. Rotated, the tree becomes the green stems of a bouquet.

Part A · 3.4Hybrid Images

The CFG velocity is predicted with two prompts at the same \(x_t\) and \(t\), and the low frequencies of one are combined with the high frequencies of the other:

$$\begin{aligned}v_1&=\text{CFG}(x_t,t,p_1)\\ v_2&=\text{CFG}(x_t,t,p_2)\\ v&=f_{\text{lowpass}}(v_1)+f_{\text{highpass}}(v_2)\end{aligned}$$

Here \(f_{\text{lowpass}}\) is a Gaussian blur and \(f_{\text{highpass}}(v)=v-f_{\text{lowpass}}(v)\). Prompt \(p_1\) sets the shape seen from far away, and \(p_2\) sets the detail seen up close.

Each hybrid is shown at full size and then shrunk, removing the fine detail which simulates viewing the image from far away.

Zebra / ocean waves. Up close it is an ink painting of crashing waves. From far away the dark wave looks like a striped zebra head and neck.

Teddy bear / flowers. Up close it is a column of pale flowers, and from far away the flower clusters merge into the outline of a teddy bear.

Part BFlow Matching from Scratch

A UNet is built and trained on MNIST digits: first as a one-step denoiser, then as time-conditioned and class-conditioned flow-matching models that generate digits from noise.

Part B · 1.1Implementing the UNet

The denoiser is a UNet. Its encoder downsamples the digit twice and averages it into a single vector, and its decoder upsamples back to full resolution. At each resolution, skip connections concatenate the encoder's features into the decoder, so fine detail does not have to pass through the bottleneck. The hidden dimension \(D\) sets how many channels each layer has.

Part B · 1.2Using the UNet to Train a Denoiser

The denoiser \(D_\theta\) learns to map a noisy image \(z\) back to a clean digit \(x\). Training pairs are made by adding Gaussian noise to clean MNIST digits, with \(x\in[0,1]\):

$$z=x+\sigma\,\epsilon,\qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$$

and the model minimizes the L2 loss \(\mathbb{E}_{z,x}\|D_\theta(z)-x\|^2\).

The digits are easy to read up to about \(\sigma=0.5\) and mostly lost at \(\sigma=1\).

Part B · 1.2.3Denoising Pure Noise

Patterns in the outputs. Every output is the same blurry gray shape, regardless of the input noise, after both epoch 1 and epoch 5. It looks like many digits stacked on top of each other, roughly a mix of 0, 3, 8 and 9.

Why. The input is independent of the target, so it says nothing about which digit to produce. Under an MSE loss, the best single prediction is the image with the smallest average squared distance to all the training digits, which is their pixel-wise mean. This is the centroid of a single cluster that contains the whole training set. The model learns to ignore its input and always output the average MNIST digit, which is why one-step denoising cannot generate new digits from pure noise.

Part B · 2.1Adding Time Conditioning to UNet

One-step denoising cannot generate, so instead a model learns the flow from noise to data and follows it in small steps. With noise \(x_0\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) and a clean digit \(x_1\), the point at time \(t\) and its velocity are

$$x_t=(1-t)\,x_0+t\,x_1,\qquad u(x_t,t)=\frac{d x_t}{dt}=x_1-x_0,$$

and the UNet \(u_\theta(x_t,t)\) is trained to predict that velocity:

$$L=\mathbb{E}_{x_0,x_1,t\sim U[0,1]}\big\|(x_1-x_0)-u_\theta(x_t,t)\big\|^2$$

The time \(t\) is passed into the UNet through small fully connected layers that scale its features, so the same network can predict the velocity at every \(t\).

To sample, start from pure noise at \(t=0\) and take small Euler steps along the predicted velocity until \(t=1\):

$$x_{t+\Delta t}=x_t+\Delta t\,u_\theta(x_t,t)$$

Part B · 2.4Adding Class-Conditioning to UNet

To improve quality and control which digit is generated, the UNet is also conditioned on the digit class \(c\in\{0,\dots,9\}\), passed in as a one-hot vector the same way as \(t\).

For classifier-free guidance the model also has to work without a class, so during training the one-hot vector is replaced by zeros 10% of the time (\(p_{\text{uncond}}=0.1\)).

Sampling runs the UNet with and without the class at each step and combines the two with classifier-free guidance at \(\gamma=5\):

$$u=u_{\text{uncond}}+\gamma\,\big(u_{\text{cond}}-u_{\text{uncond}}\big)$$

Training. Each batch pairs every clean digit \(x_1\) with fresh noise \(x_0\) and a random time \(t\), and the UNet is given \(x_t\), \(t\) and the digit's label. It is trained to predict the velocity \(x_1-x_0\). For 10% of the images the one-hot label is replaced with zeros, so the same network also learns the velocity with no class. Sampling with classifier-free guidance needs both predictions.

Loss curve. The loss starts at about 4.4, when the untrained network's output is unrelated to the target, and drops below 0.2 within the first few hundred steps. After that it decreases slowly for the rest of training, as the learning rate decays and the updates get smaller.

The loss does not go to zero. Many pairs of noise and digit pass through the same \(x_t\), and each pair has a different velocity, so the best possible prediction is their average and some error always remains. The curve also stays noisy, because every batch draws new noise and new times.

Part B · 2.6Sampling from the UNet

Four samples of each digit from 300 Euler steps with guidance \(\gamma=5\), starting from the same fixed-seed noise at every epoch.

After epoch 1 every sample is already the requested digit, though some are rough, like the 9 in the first row and some of the 2s. By epochs 5 and 10 the digits are clean, and the four samples of each digit are written in different styles.

Removing the learning rate scheduler

To remove the scheduler, a fresh model was trained with a constant learning rate of 1e-3 instead of decaying from 1e-2. Everything else is unchanged.

1e-3 is where the scheduler ends after 10 epochs (\(10^{-2}\times 0.1\)). The scheduler starts with large steps to learn quickly and ends with small steps to settle. A constant 1e-2 would keep taking large steps at the end, so the smaller rate is used the whole time instead.

The results match the scheduled model. Samples are already clean after epoch 1, and at epoch 10 they look as good as with the scheduler. The loss ends at about the same level, slightly noisier.
