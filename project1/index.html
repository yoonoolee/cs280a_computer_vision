Project 1

Images of the Russian Empire

Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii Photo Collection

Avery Lee · September 15, 2026

Sergei Mikhailovich Prokudin-Gorskii (1863–1944) traveled the Russian Empire photographing people, buildings, landscapes, railroads, bridges, and more, by recording three exposures of each scene onto a glass plate through a red, a green and a blue filter. He left Russia in 1918, right after the revolution, but his glass plate negatives survived, were purchased by the Library of Congress in 1948, and have since been digitized.

Each digitized plate holds the three exposures stacked top to bottom, in the order B, G, R. The goal is to extract the three channels, align them, and enhance the images using different methods. The full-size .tif scans are large in size, so the alignment has to be fast. All of the resulting images are collected here.

Part 1Single-Scale Alignment

cathedral.jpg
monastery.jpg
tobolsk.jpg

I start with the three small .jpg scans, where the plates are only a few pixels out of place, so a brute-force search is cheap. The idea is that the three plates show the same scene, so at the right offset their brightness patterns line up, and the best shift is the one where the two plates agree most.

  1. Read the scan and convert it to floats in \([0, 1]\).
  2. Split the image into three equal-height parts: the top is the B plate, the middle is G, the bottom is R.
  3. Keep B fixed as the anchor. For G and then for R, try every displacement \((d_x, d_y)\) in a \(\pm 20\) pixel window, and score how well it matches B.
  4. Keep the best-scoring shift, and stack the shifted R, shifted G and original B into an RGB image.

A positive \(d_x\) moves the plate to the right and a positive \(d_y\) moves it down. Two details matter for the score. First, the scans have black and white borders that are different in every plate, so the score is only computed on the interior: I ignore 5% of each edge. Second, np.roll wraps pixels that fall off one side back around to the other side, so on top of the 5% I also ignore a band as wide as the largest shift in the search window. That way the wrapped-around pixels never lands inside the scored region and can't influence the score.

I implemented two scoring metrics for each image. L2 is the Euclidean distance between the two plates, so a lower score is better. NCC (Normalized Cross-Correlation) measures similarity, so a higher score is better. With \(a\) the shifted plate and \(b\) the anchor, both already cropped to the interior and flattened into vectors:

$$\mathrm{L2}(a, b) = \sqrt{\sum_i \left(a_i - b_i\right)^2}$$
$$\mathrm{NCC}(a, b) = \left\langle \frac{a - \bar a}{\lVert a - \bar a \rVert},\; \frac{b - \bar b}{\lVert b - \bar b \rVert} \right\rangle$$

The full-size .tif scans are much larger images than the .jpg files, so their plates are off by many more pixels. A brute-force search would require a much larger candidate window, which is computationally expensive and inefficient. Instead I use a coarse-to-fine image pyramid, built with a recursive call around the single-scale implementation:

  1. If the plate is taller than 500 px, downscale both plates by a factor of 2, and recurse.
  2. At the coarsest level, run the full \(\pm 20\) px search from Part 1. This is doable since the image is smaller.
  3. Coming back up each level, double the best displacement \((d_x, d_y)\) found below and only search a \(\pm 3\) px neighborhood around it.
  4. At the finest level the refined displacement is applied to the original plate.

Which image failed, and why

Every image aligned cleanly except emir.tif. You can see a red/cyan ghost on the face, turban, and medals. The reason is his robe. It is a saturated blue, which means it is very bright on the B plate and very dark on the R plate. The assumption behind both metrics that the same scene point has similar intensity in every channel doesn't hold when the subject is strongly colored. I use a different method to fix this image in Part 4.

On the left are the five stages for emir.tif. The full pipeline was run on all 17 scans with both L2 and NCC.

4.1Better Features: Aligning on Edges Instead of Brightness

emir.tif · full frame
emir.tif · zoomed in on the face

Motivation. In Part 2, the Emir's blue robe is bright in one plate and dark in another, so no shift can make the pixel values agree. Instead of matching with intensities, we can try matching by the edges of the objects.

Approach. Before aligning, I run a Sobel filter (skimage.filters.sobel) on each plate. It returns the gradient magnitude so each pixel becomes "how sharply does brightness change here" rather than "how bright is it". A uniform area becomes 0 whether it was black or white, and an edge becomes bright whichever direction the brightness changes in. Then I run the same image pyramid search from Part 2 on the three gradient images, and finally apply the displacements it finds to the original, unfiltered plates. On the left is what the aligner sees. Its edge map is nearly identical in all three.

Results. Zooming in on the face, we can see that the image aligns better now. With gradient features, L2 and NCC picked identical offsets on all 17 images. The offsets barely moved on the other images, but the ones that did change are marked red below. All 17 images now align, including emir.

4.2Automatic Cropping

emir.tif
ilemselga.tif
harvesters.tif

Motivation. After the alignment, each stacked image has black, white, and colored borders around it. These borders should be automatically detected because their width is different on every scan and on every side.

Approach. The idea is that a border row looks nothing like the picture rows next to it, and that the change happens all at once along the whole row. So for every row (or column) I take the 70th percentile of its pixel values, separately for each channel, which gives a 3-channel "profile" down the image.

Starting 10% of the way in from each edge, where I assume it is safely picture, I walk outward toward the edge and compare each row's profile with the one \(k\) rows further in (\(k\) is 0.7% of the shorter image side, at least 3 px, so it scales with resolution). The first row where any channel jumps by more than 0.25 is where the picture ends, and everything past it is cut off. Walking from the inside out means the very first jump is the innermost border, so a white scan edge, a black plate edge and a colored fringe all get removed together in one cut. The percentile is the safety mechanism: a dark tree or a bright window inside the picture only changes a fraction of a row, so the 70th percentile hardly moves and it is not mistaken for a border, while a real border changes most of the row at once.

Results. The borders and fringes are cleanly and automatically cut off on all 17 images.

4.3Automatic White Balance

emir.tif
church.tif
wharf.tif

Motivation. Many of the aligned images have an overall tint. The plates were exposed under whatever light there was, and the three filters don't have equal sensitivity, so a white surface doesn't come out white. White balancing means estimating the color of the illuminant and then scaling the channels so that color becomes neutral.

Approach. "Gray world" assumes the average color of the scene is gray, so the per-channel mean is the illuminant; "white patch" assumes the brightest pixel is white, so the per-channel maximum is. I use the generalized mean, which is the "shades of gray" estimator, and then divide it out:

$$\ell_c = \left( \frac{1}{N} \sum_{x} I_c(x)^{p} \right)^{1/p}, \quad p = 8$$
$$I_c \leftarrow \operatorname{clip}\!\left( I_c \cdot \frac{\bar\ell}{\ell_c},\, 0,\, 1 \right), \qquad \bar\ell = \tfrac{1}{3}(\ell_R + \ell_G + \ell_B)$$

With \(p = 1\) this is exactly gray world and as \(p \to \infty\) it becomes white patch; \(p = 8\) leans on the bright pixels without being pinned by any single one. Then each channel is multiplied by a gain so that the estimated illuminant becomes a neutral gray of the same overall brightness. Any pixel that was the illuminant's color becomes gray, and everything else moves with it.

Results. Tints were slightly removed for all images. Few examples are shown. The magenta cast on emir and the blue cast on church and wharf are more removed.

4.4Better Color Mapping

emir.tif
harvesters.tif
book.tif

Motivation. So far we assumed the three plates were exactly the R, G and B channels of an RGB image. However, that is not necessarily true.

Approach. A change of color primaries is a linear map, so I mix the channels with a \(3 \times 3\) matrix:

$$\begin{bmatrix} R_{\text{new}} \\ G_{\text{new}} \\ B_{\text{new}} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.2 & -0.1 & -0.1 \\ -0.1 & 1.2 & -0.1 \\ -0.1 & -0.1 & 1.2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} R \\ G \\ B \end{bmatrix}$$

The values were tuned by hand, by trial and error on a few images, and then fixed for all of them. Each output channel keeps 120% of itself and subtracts 10% of each of the other two, which undoes the leakage between filters and pushes pure colors apart. The rows sum to 1 so the map does not fight the white balance that came before it and does not shift the overall brightness.

Results. Colors get noticeably richer without changing the neutrals: the Emir's robe and the flowers on it, the harvesters' clothes, and the red cloth under the book all separate from their surroundings more.

4.5Automatic Contrasting

emir.tif
three_generations.tif
siren.tif

Motivation. The scans don't use the full tonal range, which can make the images look hazy. The steps above also clip a few values, so it makes sense to do this last and let it use whatever range is actually left.

Approach. For each channel separately, I find the 1st and 99.5th percentiles of its values and linearly stretch them to 0 and 1, clipping anything outside:

$$I_c \leftarrow \operatorname{clip}\!\left( \frac{I_c - P_{1}(I_c)}{P_{99.5}(I_c) - P_{1}(I_c)},\; 0,\; 1 \right)$$

Using percentiles rather than the min and max is what makes this safe to run automatically: the scans have dust specks, scratches and a few blown-out pixels, and if a single one of those set the range the stretch would do nothing. Doing it per channel gives each plate its own black point, which also cleans any small residual tint that white balancing left behind.

Results. The effect is subtle on images that already used most of the range, and clearest in shadows: the boots and doorway in emir, the coats in three_generations and the leaves in siren get a more defined black.
